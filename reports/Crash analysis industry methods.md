# Industry Crash Analysis Exposes CrashIQ's Missing Layers

CrashIQ's v1 plan (pull CHP CCRS crashes for a drawn corridor, place crashes that lack coordinates, build a deterministic profile with cross-tabs, have an AI analyst write cited findings) does a solid job of the first step of standard practice: describing crash history. It leaves out the layers that engineers, reviewers and grant programs expect next. Federal practice under HSM Part B ranks sites by **Empirical Bayes (EB) expected crashes** rather than raw counts or rates, because raw numbers are distorted by regression to the mean. Federal regulation requires **both systemic and spot** safety programs. California's own funding rules differ from federal practice in ways the plan does not yet reflect. Local HSIP uses observed crashes (not EB), a closed list of **86 Caltrans countermeasures with fixed CRFs** (not CMF Clearinghouse values), 250-ft influence areas, five severity tables, and a mandatory **collision diagram and collision list**. The kickoff document explicitly defers that diagram. The research frontier (TRB ACS20, NCHRP Report 1152) is moving toward surrogate measures, connected-vehicle hard-braking data and LLM tooling, and pairs that work with a strong demand for human review and agreement with experts. CrashIQ's plan already reflects that caution. The commercial market has statewide HSM platforms costing hundreds of thousands of dollars a year, free TIMS, and a new AI-native competitor, **Citian CRASH, already deployed by SCAG** across Southern California. None of these products found generates California study-ready outputs such as HSIP Analyzer inputs, ISOAP Stage 1 safety packages or ATP crash narratives. That is CrashIQ's opening. Taking it means pulling collision diagrams into v1, replacing generic over-representation tests with the HSM's beta-binomial method, building a California-specific HSIP engine separate from the generic CMF engine, adding EB and systemic/HIN capability in v2, and resolving the "CrashIQ" name conflict before charging money.

## Federal practice ranks sites by expected, not observed, crashes

The HSM organizes safety management as a six-step cycle in Part B: network screening, diagnosis, countermeasure selection, economic appraisal, project prioritization and effectiveness evaluation ([AASHTO HSM](https://www.highwaysafetymanual.org/Pages/hsm_parts.aspx)). The key methodological point for a crash tool is that AASHTO deliberately moved screening **away from crash rates**. In AASHTO's words, rate analysis "assumes there is a linear relationship between traffic volume and the frequency of crashes," while expected-frequency methods "account for regression to the mean" and give "a more stable list of locations" ([AASHTO HSM](https://www.highwaysafetymanual.org/Pages/hsm_parts.aspx)). FHWA's network screening guide calls three methods "advanced," meaning more reliable than traditional ones: EB-adjusted expected frequency, EB-adjusted excess expected frequency, and the probability that a specific crash type exceeds a threshold proportion. It recommends running more than one measure ([FHWA screening guide](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/data-analysis-tools/systemic/screening-your-network-improve-roadway-safety-performance)). Peer-reviewed comparisons agree that "the most reliable methods are based on empirical Bayes" ([AA&P via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32036107)).

The HSM measures therefore sort into three rigor tiers, which is a useful yardstick for any product:

| Tier | Measures | Data needed | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 – Observed | Crash frequency, crash rate, critical rate, EPDO, relative severity index | Crashes (plus AADT for rates) | Biased by regression to the mean; rates assume linearity |
| 2 – SPF, no EB | Excess predicted frequency, LOSS | Crashes, AADT, SPF | Ignores the site's own history |
| 3 – Best practice | EB expected, EB excess expected, EPDO with EB | Crashes, AADT, inventory, calibrated SPF | Needs volumes and SPFs |
| Volume-free alternative | Probability of specific crash type exceeding threshold proportion; excess proportion | Crash type and location only | Ignores volume; can flag sites that have few non-target crashes |

The threshold-proportion test is the one HSM method that needs no volumes and is "not affected by regression-to-the-mean bias" ([FHWA screening guide](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/data-analysis-tools/systemic/screening-your-network-improve-roadway-safety-performance)). That matters directly to CrashIQ v1, which has no AADT unless the user types it in.

**Prediction** lives in HSM Part C. A base-condition safety performance function (SPF), a function of AADT, length or entering volumes, is adjusted for site conditions and calibrated locally. The result is then combined with the site's observed crashes through EB weighting ([AASHTO HSM](https://www.highwaysafetymanual.org/Pages/hsm_parts.aspx)). EB, which dates to Hauer et al. (2002), remains "the workhorse of highway safety analysis" ([arXiv 2605.27889](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27889)). The second edition (HSM2) is imminent but **not confirmed published as of October 2026**. FHWA said publication was "likely… in 2026," a TRB webinar in March 2026 still said "anticipated in 2026," and AASHTO's catalog still lists only HSM1 with its 2014 supplement ([FHWA IHSDM plans](https://highways.dot.gov/media/110891); [TRB Webinar 1905](https://webinar.mytrb.org/Webinars/Details/1905); [AASHTO catalog](https://downloads.transportation.org/publications/catalogs/aashto_designsafety_catalog.pdf)).

HSM2 changes the target for any predictive tool. It adds new total, KABC and KAB SPFs, pedestrian and bicycle SPFs, directional freeway models, new intersection types and roundabouts, an areawide (macro) chapter, and a **systemic approach chapter** that covers "allocating funds between systemic and crash-based projects." It also switches to the term "adjustment factors" inside Part C, keeping "CMF" for Part D and Clearinghouse values ([AASHTO HSM2 webinar](https://www.highwaysafetymanual.org/Documents/AASHTO_HSM2_Update_webinar_20240618_Combined.pdf)). FHWA expects agencies to need transition time to calibrate the new models ([FHWA IHSDM plans](https://highways.dot.gov/media/110891)). Caltrans is among roughly 25 states sponsoring the $1.57M HSM2 implementation pooled fund ([TRB RIP 2169804](https://rip.trb.org/View/2169804)).

The regulation sets a minimum that a pure hot-spot tool cannot meet. 23 CFR 924.9 requires states to develop "a comprehensive program of **systemic and spot** safety improvement projects." It also requires prioritization by fatal and serious-injury reduction potential and cost-effectiveness, and roadway data meeting the MIRE Fundamental Data Elements ([eCFR 23 CFR 924.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-23/section-924.9)). The systemic approach finds locations by **risk factors associated with severe crash types**, not by crash counts. FHWA says this is the right method where sites "may not have enough crash history to reveal crash patterns" ([FHWA Step 4 Diagnose](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/local-rural/improving-safety-rural-local-and-tribal-roads-safety-toolkit/step-4-diagnose)). FHWA's 2024 Systemic Safety User Guide replaced the 2013 SSPST ([FHWA](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/data-analysis-tools/systemic/systemic-safety-user-guide)).

On the software side, IHSDM development is discontinued; its 2021 release is final ([FHWA IHSDM plans](https://highways.dot.gov/media/110891)). **AASHTOWare Safety (powered by Numetric)** is now the de facto benchmark for HSM-compliant screening. It offers segment and sliding-window screening with "Historical, Predictive, and Expected performance measures," custom SPFs, collision diagrams and Part C alternatives analysis ([AASHTOWare Segment](https://aashtoware.org/products/safety/aashtoware-safety-segment); [Alternative Design](https://aashtoware.org/products/safety/safety-alternative-design)).

Federal policy messaging also shifted in 2025–2026. FHWA withdrew its Safe System HSIP rulemaking in May 2025 ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/05/30/2025-09726/highway-safety-improvement-program-withdrawal)). In July 2026 it removed five items from the Proven Safety Countermeasures web list: road diets, bike lanes, appropriate speed limits, speed safety cameras and variable speed limits ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/16/nx-s1-5893672/dot-bike-lanes-speed-cameras-safety-measures)). A revised National Roadway Safety Strategy in August 2026 then dropped "Safer Speeds" ([Eno Center](https://enotrans.org/article/trump-administration-safety-approach-varies-widely-from-past-strategies)). The technical base (HSM, CMF Clearinghouse, 23 CFR 924) did not change; the Clearinghouse was last updated September 25, 2026 ([CMF Clearinghouse](https://cmfclearinghouse.fhwa.dot.gov/)). A pattern library tied to the current FHWA PSC web list would therefore be tied to the least stable source in the field.

## California rewards observed crashes, fixed CRFs and complete paperwork

California's local practice deliberately departs from HSM best practice, and CrashIQ's California modes have to follow California's rules rather than the HSM's. Caltrans "has avoided using methodology requiring agencies to mathematically adjust their crash data (e.g., Empirical Bayes) and instead has opted to use 3 to 5 years of 'observed crashes' in estimating 'expected crashes.'" The reason given is that "the lack of reliable statewide traffic volumes for all roadway types precludes Caltrans from using the crash rate methodology" in HSIP ranking ([LRSM v1.7](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2024/lrsm2024-v2.pdf)). The LRSM's analysis core is **crash frequency and crash rate**, using the FHWA formulas (crashes per 100 million VMT for segments, per million entering vehicles for intersections). It recommends "at least 5 years of network-wide crash data," plus pattern review by type, severity, mode, pavement, time of day and control ([LRSM v1.7](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2024/lrsm2024-v2.pdf)). LRSM **Version 1.8 (May 11, 2026)** now governs Cycle 13 and supersedes v1.7 figures ([Cycle 13 call page](https://dot.ca.gov/programs/local-assistance/fed-and-state-programs/highway-safety-improvement-program/apply-now); [Cycle 13 Q&A](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-qa-20260721.pdf)).

**HSIP Cycle 13 is a fully deterministic benefit-cost exercise**, which is exactly what CrashIQ's code-computes-every-number architecture is built to do. Applicants choose from **86 countermeasures with fixed CRFs**: 22 signalized-intersection, 25 non-signalized and 39 roadway. Each CRF is scoped to one crash subset: All (63), Ped & Bike (18), Night (3), Emergency Vehicle (1) or Animal (1) ([Cycle 13 slides](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-slides-20260721.pdf)). CRFs stack multiplicatively, with each later CRF applied to the crashes remaining after the earlier ones. Annual benefit sums CRF × crashes × crash cost by severity, divided by the number of data years, and the BCR is life benefit divided by project cost. Crash costs in 2026 dollars put KA (fatal plus serious injury, combined since Cycle 7 to dampen randomness) at **$2.379M at signalized intersections, $3.784M at non-signalized intersections and $3.276M on roadways**, against $213k for B, $121k for C and $20k for PDO ([HSIP Analyzer Manual 2026](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/hsipanalyzermanual2026bcrfinalapp.pdf)).

The input rules are just as specific. The crash period must be **3–5 years**, optionally split to skip the **2020–2022 COVID years** ([Cycle 13 Q&A](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-qa-20260721.pdf)). Each crash counts once at its most severe outcome, and sub-dataset counts cannot exceed the All dataset. Influence areas are fixed: all crashes **within 250 ft** of a new signal; for other intersection work, crashes within 250 ft in the affected directions, but only to mid-block when the next intersection is **closer than 500 ft**; and the improvement limits for longitudinal treatments. Extensions need written justification, and crashes not reported by law enforcement are excluded ([HSIP Analyzer Manual 2026](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/hsipanalyzermanual2026bcrfinalapp.pdf)). Applications must attach a **collision diagram** and a matching **collision list** with excluded crashes crossed out, because reviewers look for "a pattern of collisions… susceptible to being reduced by the countermeasure" ([Cycle 13 Application Instructions](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/applicationinstructions2026.pdf)).

The minimum BCR to apply is **5.0**, but recent funding cutoffs were much higher:

| Cycle | Applications | Selected | $M | BCR cutoff | Avg BCR selected |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 (2018) | 351 | 220 | 180.8 | 7.5 | 17.7 |
| 10 (2021) | 429 | 268 | 238.3 | 12.0 | 24.0 |
| 11 (2023) | 434 | 286 | 231.6 | 18.0 | 35.5 |
| 12 (2025) | 330 | 288 | 299.6 | 7.0 | 28.3 |

Source: [Cycle 13 slides](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-slides-20260721.pdf). Cycle 13 offers $370M and closes **November 2, 2026**. Caltrans keeps bike lanes and road diets on its list even though USDOT removed them from its PSC list; its guidance is "go with the appendix" ([Cycle 13 Q&A](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-qa-20260721.pdf)). This is concrete evidence that California and federal countermeasure lists have diverged.

On the State Highway System, **ISOAP (September 2024) replaced the 2013 ICE policy** with a two-stage, Safe-System-based process. Stage 1's initial safety assessment allows "the SPICE tool, Caltrans [TASAS] rate groups, crash modification factors, or other methods," alongside FHWA's Safe System for Intersections (SSI) score covering conflict points, kinetic energy and movement complexity, so **SPICE is permitted, not mandated**. Stage 2 predicts crash frequency and severity per strategy, using the HSM where applicable, and converts the result to a BCR with Caltrans crash costs. ISOAP applies to new intersections or major modifications on the SHS, including new connections or driveways with ADT of 1,000 or more and encroachment-permit projects, which makes it a real trigger for development studies ([ISOAP Guide](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/traffic-operations/documents/isoap/20240910-isoap-process-info-guide--a11y.pdf)).

SPICE itself is an FHWA Excel macro whose calibration factors default to 1.0 ([SPICE User Manual](https://cmfclearinghouse.fhwa.dot.gov/collateral/FHWA-SA-18-026%20SPICE%20Tool%20User%20Guide%20(Final).pdf)). For SHS facilities, Caltrans publishes average base crash rates and crash costs for **31 intersection rate groups**, plus highway and ramp tables, on data.ca.gov (last updated August 2026) ([CA Open Data](https://lab.data.ca.gov/dataset/2024-crash-data-on-state-highway-system/07f78e23-2381-4e59-a0e7-92f0c64022fe)). Caltrans warns that only these base rates are refined enough for comparison ([Caltrans 2021 crash data book](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/research-innovation-system-information/documents/annual-collision-data/2021crashdataoncshwybook1a11y.pdf)). **No equivalent statewide average exists for local roads.**

The data layer changed in 2025. CHP shut off public iSWITRS on January 8, 2025, and CCRS on data.ca.gov became the raw source: per-year Crashes, Parties and InjuredWitnessPassengers files for 2016–2026, updated daily, public domain ([CHP](https://chp.ca.gov/iswtrs); [data.ca.gov CCRS](https://data.ca.gov/dataset/ccrs)). TIMS remains the practitioner default. It geocodes about **97%** of SWITRS crashes using an Esri locator plus officer offset, with TSN postmile referencing on state highways ([TIMS geocoding](https://tims.berkeley.edu/help/crashdata/switrs-geocoding.php)). The LRSM notes that TIMS excludes property-damage-only crashes ([LRSM v1.7](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2024/lrsm2024-v2.pdf)). CrashIQ's own spike found that only **78% of 2024 CCRS crashes carry coordinates**, falling to **33–57% in Sacramento, San Francisco, San Diego and LA City**, the urban corridors where most studies happen ([CrashIQ CCRS spike](file:///Users/Ak47/Desktop/Crash/docs/02-spike-ccrs.md)). Police data also undercount severe injuries. San Francisco's hospital-linked records show police KSI is a lower bound ([SFCTA 2022 HIN](https://www.sfcta.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/SFCTA_Board_SFDPHHighInjuryNetwork_2022-11-15.pdf)).

Planning-level work in California increasingly means **high-injury networks (HINs) under LRSP and SS4A**. Local HSIP eligibility requires an LRSP or equivalent (SSAR, Vision Zero plan or SS4A Action Plan) updated within five years. Caltrans no longer funds LRSPs ([Caltrans LRSP page](https://dot.ca.gov/programs/local-assistance/fed-and-state-programs/highway-safety-improvement-program/local-roadway-safety-plans)). SS4A requires a safety analysis covering "all roadways within the jurisdiction, without regard for ownership," including systemic analysis and "a high-injury network or equivalent" ([USDOT SS4A](https://www.transportation.gov/grants/ss4a/comprehensive-safety-action-plans)). There is no single California HIN method:

| City | HIN method | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| San Francisco | Linked police, hospital and death records; overlapping quarter-mile sections; 10 KSI per mile threshold | 13.3% of street miles, 74% of KSI | [SF 2024 HIN](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/2024_Vision_Zero_Network_Update_Methodology_For_Steering_Review_20260804_V.4_ca_for_HC.pdf) |
| Los Angeles | Mode-weighted KSI with equity tracts and 16 systemic risk factors | 549 miles (7.5% of streets), 61.8% of KSI | [LADOT 2024](https://ladot.lacity.gov/sites/default/files/documents/la-vision-zero-safety-study-2024.pdf) |
| San Diego | Annual high-crash lists, rate ≥ mean + 1 SD, only intersections with ≥5 crashes, equity labels | — | [San Diego 2024 memo](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-03/2024-high-crash-locations.pdf) |

For traffic studies, SB 743 made VMT the CEQA metric from July 2020. Caltrans promised a "simplified safety analysis approach" focused on multimodal conflicts and access management ([Caltrans TISG 2020](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/transportation-planning/documents/sb-743/2020-05-20-approved-vmt-focused-tisg-a11y.pdf)). A TIS safety section is therefore a review item, not a CEQA significance test. ATP Cycle 8 scores safety on "the history of pedestrian and bicycle collisions and the selected countermeasures" ([CTC 2027 ATP Guidelines](https://catc.ca.gov/-/media/ctc-media/documents/programs/atp/cycle8/2027-atp-guidelines-a11y.pdf)).

## Diagnosis remains a checklist that engineers must verify

HSM Chapter 5 defines diagnosis in three steps: a safety data review, a review of supporting documentation, and a field assessment. Its descriptive-statistics fields (date, day and time; crash type; KABCO severity; direction of travel; parties involved; road surface, lighting, weather and impairment) are effectively a specification for CrashIQ's cross-tab dimensions. The HSM suggests **3 to 5 years** of data "to improve the reliability of the diagnosis" ([HSM Ch. 5 draft](https://ftp.granit.unh.edu/submissions/NHDOTOUT/craig/HSM/Chapter%205_Diagnosis.pdf)). Collision diagrams are central. They are a plan view of crash type and direction, which the HSM says is more efficiently produced "with software" for multiple sites, and are paired with a field-drawn condition diagram. The HSM flags a location-coding pitfall: crashes coded to the intersection centerline "may misrepresent actual crash locations" ([HSM Ch. 5 draft](https://ftp.granit.unh.edu/submissions/NHDOTOUT/craig/HSM/Chapter%205_Diagnosis.pdf)).

Chapter 6 maps crash patterns to "possible contributing factors" by facility type. The draft exhibits cover segments, signalized and unsignalized intersections, rail crossings, pedestrians and bicyclists. Their stated purpose is to "minimize the probability that a major contributing factor will be overlooked." The HSM insists that linking factors to countermeasures "requires engineering judgment and local knowledge" ([HSM Ch. 6 draft](https://ftp.granit.unh.edu/submissions/NHDOTOUT/craig/HSM/Chapter%206_SelectingCountermeasures.pdf)). FHWA reinforces this: "most crashes cannot be related to a singular causal event" ([FHWA Step 4](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/local-rural/improving-safety-rural-local-and-tribal-roads-safety-toolkit/step-4-diagnose)). CrashIQ's pattern library therefore has to output *candidates for field verification*, not diagnosed causes.

**The only HSM-sanctioned over-representation test is a beta-binomial method**: the "Probability of Specific Crash Types Exceeding Threshold Proportion," with its extension "Excess Proportion." Sites are grouped into reference populations of similar sites (for example signalized versus two-way stop-controlled). The site proportion is computed only where the site has **two or more target crashes**, and the threshold is the pooled proportion across the reference population. Beta parameters come from the sample variance, sites are ranked by P(p_i > p*), and excess proportion is reported above a limiting probability such as 90% ([HSM Ch. 4 draft](https://ftp.granit.unh.edu/submissions/NHDOTOUT/craig/HSM/Chapter%204_NetworkScreening.pdf)). The HSM allows the test for "collision type or crash severity," so the same calculation works for night, wet, ped/bike or KA shares. Its known weaknesses are that it ignores volume and can flag a site simply because it has few non-target crashes. Oregon DOT therefore pairs it with critical crash rate ([ODOT APM App. 4A](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/Planning/Documents/APMv2_App4A.pdf)). A Utah-related FHWA report notes that proportion tests cannot detect elevated severity within a crash type ([ROSAP 88627](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/88627/dot_88627_DS1.pdf)). No HSM, FHWA or Caltrans source prescribes chi-square or z-tests, or multiple-comparison corrections across cross-tab cells. A tool that uses them is going beyond standard practice and must say so.

Several machine-readable countermeasure sources exist. **LRSM v1.8 Appendix B** gives each countermeasure "where to use" text that works as a symptom trigger; intersection lighting, for example, is indicated where there is "a disproportionate number of night-time crashes" ([HSIP Analyzer Manual 2026](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/hsipanalyzermanual2026bcrfinalapp.pdf)). **FHWA's FCFT guide** maps focus crash types to contributing factors and then to countermeasures, while warning that a predictive approach "is more reliable than an approach based only on cross-tabulations" ([FHWA-HRT-20-053](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-TD2-PURL-gpo154695/pdf/GOVPUB-TD2-PURL-gpo154695.pdf)). **PEDSAFE** maps 12 PBCAT crash-type groups to causes and countermeasures ([PEDSAFE](https://pedbikesafe.org/PEDSAFE/guide_analysis_CrashTypeAnalysis.cfm)), and **STEP** keys pedestrian-crossing treatments to lanes, speed and AADT ([FHWA STEP](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/43674/dot_43674_DS1.pdf)). The **CMF Clearinghouse** rates CMFs from 1 to 5 stars on a 150-point scale, where 3 stars needs at least 75 points ([CMF Clearinghouse SQR](https://cmfclearinghouse.fhwa.dot.gov/sqr.php)).

On combining CMFs, FHWA research found that multiplication "overestimates the actual reduction in a majority of instances" and recommended "that no more than two CMFs be combined" ([FHWA Combining CMFs](https://cmfclearinghouse.fhwa.dot.gov/collateral/Combining_Multiple_CMFs_Final.pdf)). Caltrans HSIP takes a different line. It multiplies sequentially and allows up to three countermeasures per category (SI, NS, R), so up to nine per project ([Cycle 13 Q&A, Q29](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2026/cycle13-webinar-qa-20260721.pdf)). A countermeasure engine therefore needs separate California and generic rule sets.

Pulled together, these sources define what a **defensible finding** should carry: the data period, the crash-location assignment rule, the reference population and threshold, the test statistic and limiting probability, the guideline row used for each contributing factor, the CMF ID and star rating, and explicit items requiring field verification ([HSM Ch. 5](https://ftp.granit.unh.edu/submissions/NHDOTOUT/craig/HSM/Chapter%205_Diagnosis.pdf); [LRSM](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/local-assistance/documents/hsip/2024/lrsm2024-v2.pdf); [CMF Clearinghouse](https://cmfclearinghouse.fhwa.dot.gov/sqr.php)). No single published reviewer checklist exists. This list is compiled from method guidance.

## Research is shifting to leading indicators and audited AI

The January conference in Washington is the TRB Annual Meeting (105th, January 11–15, 2026) ([National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/events/886)). The crash-analytics committees are **ACS20 Safety Performance and Analysis** (SPFs, EB, screening, CMFs, surrogates, crash data), **ACS10 Transportation Safety Management Systems** (programs and policy, with a Caltrans member) and **AED60 Statistical and Econometric Methods**. ACS20's free annual synthesis is the best map of the field ([104th Synthesis](https://trbacs20.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/104th-TRBAM-Synthesis-Report-on-Safety-Related-Papers.pdf)). In 2025, papers clustered in crash data and analysis (176), severity prediction (93), connected and automated vehicle effects (77) and surrogate measures (62). Only 18 were on network screening and 15 on SPFs ([104th Synthesis](https://trbacs20.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/104th-TRBAM-Synthesis-Report-on-Safety-Related-Papers.pdf)). SPF/EB practice is mature. The new work is in leading indicators and machine learning.

Five research fronts matter for CrashIQ. The first is **surrogate safety measures and connected-vehicle hard braking**. Google Research linked Android Auto hard-braking events to police-reported crashes in **California (from CCRS)** and Virginia using negative binomial regression. Hard-braking events appeared on 18× more segments than reported crashes did, and the hard-braking rate was significantly associated with crash rate ([arXiv 2601.06327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06327), preprint; [Google blog](https://research.google/blog/hard-braking-events-as-indicators-of-road-segment-crash-risk)). The field has not yet accepted this for agency decisions. NCHRP 17-149, on whether hard braking is reliable as a surrogate, is still an unawarded FY2027 project ([TRB](http://apps.trb.org/cmsfeed/TRBNetProjectDisplay.asp?ProjectID=6054)). The second is **LLMs on crash narratives**: a fine-tuned RoBERTa model reached F1 0.90 on secondary-crash identification, against 0.86 for zero-shot LLaMA3-70B ([arXiv 2508.04399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04399), preprint). An IEEE ITSC 2025 paper found that higher-accuracy models often agreed *less* with experts, and argued for Cohen's kappa and expert-agreement metrics ([arXiv 2504.13068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13068)). The remaining three are **Safe System kinetic-energy metrics** such as the Kinetic Velocity Index for FSI probability, which builds on FHWA's SSI ([TRID 2652210](https://trid.trb.org/View/2652210)); **equity disparity analysis** ([FHWA-HRT-25-035](https://highways.dot.gov/sites/fhwa.dot.gov/files/FHWA-HRT-25-035.pdf)); and **ML frequency and severity models paired with SHAP** for interpretability ([AA&P 2022](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001457522000537)).

The authoritative AI benchmark is **NCHRP Research Report 1152 (2025)**, *Leveraging AI and Big Data to Enhance Safety Analysis*. It sets out a requirements-first, seven-step ML design process. It warns that decision trees are unstable and SVM/KNN models opaque, and that neural networks transfer poorly between sites. Its example workflow has an engineer double-checking ML output ([NCHRP 1152](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/29098/9780309599603_508.pdf)).

The foundational warning still applies: prediction accuracy and causal validity trade off in safety models ([Mannering et al. 2020](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213665720300038)). ML can serve as a secondary flag. SPF/EB stays authoritative, and an AI write-up must never present an association as a CMF. CrashIQ's design (code computes every number, findings cite tables, the user approves) already matches NCHRP 1152's posture. What it lacks is an expert-agreement metric in its evaluation.

## Competitors own dashboards and maps, not California study outputs

The market falls into four groups. **Statewide HSM platforms** are led by AASHTOWare Safety/Numetric, which Arizona DOT says 16 states use ([AZ PIJ](https://aset.az.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/DT25009%20PIJ%20FINAL%20082025.pdf)). **Local crash-records and collision-diagram tools** include Crash Magic, Crossroads and TIMS; Crash Magic's maker also sells Road Position Magic, a paid service that geocodes crashes by walking them from the nearest intersection by distance and direction ([pdmagic RPM](https://pdmagic.com/gis/rpm)), which is the commercial analogue of CrashIQ's J1 placement step. **Big-data platforms** added LLM agents in 2026: INRIX Compass in March and the Replica + Arity Safety Hub in June ([INRIX](https://inrix.com/press-releases/inrix-launches-compass-ai-platform-to-help-cities-reduce-systemic-roadway-safety-risk/); [Replica](https://replicahq.com/safety-hub)). **Video and lidar near-miss analytics** come from Miovision, Derq and TrafxSAFE ([Miovision](https://miovision.com/safety-studies/); [Derq](https://en.derq.com/press-coverage/derq-advances-traffic-safety-intelligence-with-next-generation-of-insight)). Kittelson's crashKIT runs HSM screening measures without GIS skills ([crashKIT](https://crashkit.kittelsonsoftware.com/)).

Prices span a wide range. AASHTOWare Safety in California's pricing tier (Group 3) lists at **$385k per year per module** for Segment and Intersection, plus $25k implementation units ([AASHTOWare FY2027 Catalog](https://www.aashtoware.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FY-2027-AASHTOWare_Catalog_web.pdf)). Urban SDK's municipal contracts run about **$8.5k–$35k per year** ([Imperial, CA](https://motioncount.com/items/imperialCA_e5e356ac_2575); [Olmsted Falls](https://cleveland.com/community/2026/03/olmsted-falls-considering-modern-police-department-gps-speeding-software-service.html)). TIMS, the HSIP Analyzer and SPICE are free. No seat-priced, consultant-targeted California crash product was found. CrashIQ's real competitor is "free TIMS + Excel + staff hours."

**Citian CRASH is the direct threat.** It advertises AI and NLP data cleanup, collision diagrams, an HSM-tied countermeasure engine ranked by B/C, HSIP screening dashboards and "grant-ready outputs" ([Citian](https://www.citian.co/crash)). SCAG is rolling it out to "SCAG and partner agencies, including cities, counties, and consultants working on their behalf" ([SCAG Toolbox Tuesday](https://scag.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2025-02/tt_jan2025_part1_combined.pdf)). That may make it free to CrashIQ's natural Southern California buyers. Its public pages do not claim to produce Caltrans HSIP Analyzer inputs, ISOAP packages or ATP formats.

AASHTOWare sells services to "automate reports that currently require consultative engagements" ([AASHTOWare catalog](https://www.aashtoware.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FY-2027-AASHTOWare_Catalog_web.pdf)). That confirms agencies pay for recurring report writing. None of the products found writes evidence-cited narrative findings from crash records in a study-report format.

**The name "CrashIQ" is already in use** by an open-source MCP project (US accident-severity ML) and a Blink-built AI accident-documentation app ([LobeHub](https://lobehub.com/mcp/sandeeppudi-crashiq-road-accident-severity-intelligence-platform); [Blink](https://blink.new/p/crashiq-ai-platform-qxnij27p)). Neither is a traffic engineering product, but both are a trademark and search issue for a paid launch.

## CrashIQ's plan covers the profile but misses five standard layers

Set against the industry, the plan's real strengths are **fresher data** (direct CCRS, including the partial current year, which TIMS publishes later) and **placement of crashes that lack coordinates**. That is a problem Pd' sells as a separate service, and the spike shows it hits urban CCRS hardest. Its third strength is the **auditable AI analyst**, which no competitor offers in study format. The gaps are concentrated in what happens after the profile.

## Conclusion

The research changes what CrashIQ's main bet should be. The plan assumes the value lies in the AI analyst that writes findings. In California, the parts a buyer most clearly pays for are deterministic and rule-bound: an HSIP crash table that the Analyzer will accept, a collision diagram that reviewers require, an ISOAP Stage 1 package for a driveway permit, and a HIN that satisfies SS4A. Those outputs are exactly where incumbents are weakest. AASHTOWare is priced for states, TIMS stops at maps and diagrams, and Citian targets regional dashboards. The AI analyst becomes credible only on top of HSM-correct statistics (a reference-population proportion test, regression-to-the-mean caveats and, eventually, EB). Without them, a fluent narrative over raw cross-tabs is the "outdated crash rate analysis" HSM2's authors are trying to retire.

There is also a strategic tension to manage. California's HSIP rewards observed crashes and fixed CRFs, while federal best practice and HSM2 reward expected crashes and calibrated SPFs. A product that keeps those two modes explicitly separate will look unusually rigorous to reviewers. It would show an observed-crash HSIP BCR next to an EB-based "is this site actually high-risk?" view, and say which is which. A product that blends them will be wrong for both. The fastest route to a defensible paid launch is to ship v1 with diagrams and the HSM proportion test, bring the California HSIP engine forward ahead of SPICE, and rename the product before the first invoice.
