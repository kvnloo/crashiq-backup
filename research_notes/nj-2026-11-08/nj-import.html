New Jersey: every reported crash, 2017–2023

In one line: New Jersey posts every police-reported crash, one file per county and year, no login, with each person's injury level (K A B C O) and street names. Only 26–38% of crashes before 2023 have a map point (60% in 2023), the newest year is 2023, and there's no licence that says a paid tool may use it.

1. What New Jersey has

WhatExample
Crashes195,000–284,000 a year (2023: 247,701).
Map point2017–2022: 26–38%. 2023: 60% (Newark's county: 40%).
Street namesRoad and cross street with distance on 76–82%: DEMOTT LANE, 101 ft E of 15TH ST.
Injury levelPer person: fatal, serious, minor, possible, none = K A B C O.
Missing2024 onward (only in NJDOT's online portal). Current-dollar crash costs.

2. Does it place without GPS? Partly

82–89%
of crashes CrashIQ placed from the street names alone (GPS hidden) landed within 250 ft of the state's point (Newark's county 82%, New Brunswick's 89%). It places 62–74% of them; a crash written only as ESSEX COUNTY 669 can't be placed, because the Census map doesn't number those county roads.

3. A real corridor

Broad Street, Market to Bridge Street, Newark, 1.01 mi, 2020–2023 (2024 not posted): 405 crashes: 2 fatal, 7 serious, 24 minor, 58 possible, 314 property damage; 198 more couldn't be placed. No dollar total (New Jersey's manual gives 2001 costs with no update rule). PDF and Excel exports work.

What you need to do

  1. Ask NJDOT's Bureau of Transportation Data and Safety in writing if a paid tool may load the crash tables, and what notice to show (their pages cite 23 USC 407).
  2. Pick how to bring the 2001 crash costs to today's dollars, or leave costs off.
  3. Ask NJDOT when the 2024 tables will be posted.
  4. Say if we should build placement from route + milepost (on ~90% of NJ crashes): it would place most crashes without a point.

What we didn't check

2001–2016 files, house addresses, contributing-circumstance and alcohol flags.
